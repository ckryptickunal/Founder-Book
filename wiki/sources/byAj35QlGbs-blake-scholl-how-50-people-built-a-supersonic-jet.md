# Blake Scholl: How 50 People Built a Supersonic Jet

## Summary

Blake Scholl, CEO of Boom Supersonic, discusses the stagnation of air travel innovation since the 1960s and his company's mission to bring back supersonic passenger flight. He details Boom's journey, from overcoming initial skepticism and regulatory hurdles (like the US ban on supersonic flight) to achieving the first independent supersonic flight with XB1, which also demonstrated a solution to the sonic boom problem. Scholl emphasizes Boom's innovative approach to hardware development, treating it like software with rapid iteration cycles, vertical integration, and leveraging AI. He also shares insights on entrepreneurship, manufacturing, hiring, and the importance of passion and purpose in building impactful ventures.

## Key Ideas

- Air travel innovation has stagnated since the 1960s, with modern airliners offering no speed improvement.
- Increased travel speed has historically driven significant societal and economic changes, and a new supersonic age promises similar transformations.
- Startups can disrupt entrenched industries like aerospace by challenging conventional wisdom and adopting new methodologies.
- Boom Supersonic treats hardware development like software development, using rapid iteration, custom software tools (Makeboom, Bladeunner), and vertical integration to reduce costs and accelerate progress.
- The XB1 prototype successfully demonstrated a solution to the sonic boom problem ('mock cutoff'), which was crucial for changing US regulations and legalizing supersonic flight.
- Financing deep tech ventures requires innovative strategies, such as adapting core technology (supersonic engines) for other markets (ground power generation via Superpower turbines).
- Passion, purpose, and self-belief are critical for entrepreneurs, especially in deep tech with long development cycles and high skepticism.
- America can compete in manufacturing by inventing the next generation of production processes (e.g., tooling-free digital manufacturing) rather than trying to repatriate old methods.
- AI is reducing the cost of software development, enabling more custom engineering tools and allowing users to become tool builders, though physical world AI for manufacturing is still nascent.
- Hiring for deep tech requires valuing early career talent, hands-on builders, and people who care, often promoting from within to maintain culture.

## Notable Claims

- Half a century later, we can't go to the moon and we can't fly faster than the speed of sound. Evidence: Contrasts 1969 achievements (moon landing, Concord) with current capabilities.
- It now takes Lockheed more than two years to build a new Patriot missile interceptor. This is crazy. Evidence: Wall Street Journal article from a couple weeks prior.
- The Boeing 787... doesn't fly any faster. It doesn't make the world any more accessible. Evidence: Direct comparison to the Boeing 707, noting no speed improvement over half a century.
- Hawaii was simply not a tourist destination before the speed of the jet. Evidence: The ability to get from the US to Honolulu in 7-9 hours transformed it into a mainstream tourist destination.
- The Beatles took the First World Tour in the late 1960s, a tour that simply would not have been practical 10 years earlier with slower airplanes. Evidence: Implied by the increased speed of jet travel making global tours feasible.
- One of the fallouts of the Concord era was that we actually banned supersonic flight in the US. Evidence: Stated as a historical fact and a regulatory hurdle for supersonic private jets.
- XB1 became the first independently developed jet to break the sound barrier. Evidence: Video footage and announcement of the successful flight in the Mojave Desert.
- XB1 broke the sound barrier on two flights, six times through the sound barrier. Each time no audible sonic boom in the ground. Evidence: Demonstrated the 'mock cutoff' principle by flying at high altitude and right speed.
- It was about 24 hours from breaking the sound barrier to be invited to the West Wing and 115 days from breaking the sound barrier to an executive order making supersonic flight legal again in the US. Evidence: Timeline of events following XB1's successful flight.
- Supersonic flight isn't red or blue. It's red, white, and blue. Evidence: A bill to make legalization permanent passed the House unanimously and the Senate Commerce Committee unanimously.
- The XP1 team was just 50 incredibly committed, passionate men and women. Evidence: Stated as the actual team size for building the prototype.
- The key thing is to make hardware development look more like software development to reduce the cost of iteration both in the world of bits and in the world of atoms. Evidence: Boom's internal tools like Makeboom and Bladeunner, and their in-house machine shop.
- This is a room that does not exist at GE or Rolls-Royce or Boeing or any other large aerospace company. Evidence: Description of Boom's R&D machine shop in Denver, enabling 24-hour digital design to prototype.
- We were able to take the engine that we were building for supersonic flight and sell it separately on the ground as a natural gas power turbine and that's what we call superpower. Evidence: Description of the Superpower product and its dual purpose for financing and testing.
- The technology for supersonic flight existed actually a long time before boom was founded. Technologically the company could have been created 10 years earlier but nobody started it. Evidence: Scholl's personal reflection on the 'why now' question, suggesting many solvable problems are overlooked.
- We don't win [against China in manufacturing] in a game where the most labor is what matters... We need to invent the next generation of manufacturing scale that here because what America is still best at is invention and freedom and innovation. Evidence: Boom's development of all-digital, tooling-free manufacturing processes for turbine blades.
- AI has dramatically reduced the cost of software development. Evidence: Allows for custom engineering tools that would have been unaffordable previously.
- The last company entrepreneur founded that went on to build a commercial airliner was Douglas aircraft in 1921. Evidence: Historical fact highlighting the rarity of aerospace startups.
- The approvals process for XB1 that even for an R&D airplane could stretch on for months actually happened in 90 minutes. Evidence: Result of Boom's strategy of early and collaborative engagement with the FAA.
- The moment you become an expert, what you're steeped in is the past and then you're completely useless. Evidence: Scholl's strong opinion on the dangers of over-specialization and resistance to new ideas.
