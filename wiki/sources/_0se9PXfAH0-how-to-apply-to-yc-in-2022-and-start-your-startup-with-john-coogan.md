# How to apply to YC in 2022 and start your startup (with John Coogan)

## Metadata

## Summary

Garry Tan and John Coogan discuss the merits of starting a startup and applying to Y Combinator (YC). John Coogan, co-founder of Soylent and a two-time YC alumnus, shares his entrepreneurial journey, including his initial failed startup, the pivot to Soylent, and the lessons learned in product validation and controversial marketing. The conversation also delves into the YC application process, emphasizing the importance of founder commitment, complementary team skills, and the need for both urgency and mastery in building a startup. Both hosts encourage aspiring founders to take risks and continuously learn.

## Key Ideas

- Starting a startup and applying to Y Combinator can be life-changing and lead to significant personal and professional growth.
- Early career choices, such as joining a startup versus a large tech company, can have long-term impacts, with regret often stemming from missed opportunities for high-risk, high-reward ventures.
- Successful startups often emerge from solving personal problems efficiently, as exemplified by Soylent's origin in addressing founders' food needs.
- Effective marketing can leverage controversy and 'niching down' to generate significant earned media and build a dedicated community, even if it alienates a larger audience.
- Y Combinator prioritizes founder commitment and a 'prove you're wrong' mentality over de-risking strategies like conditional co-founder involvement.
- The YC application process is a valuable tool for clarifying business thinking, requiring founders to articulate their vision, importance, scalability, and current traction concisely.
- A strong founding team possesses complementary skills necessary to achieve initial milestones, and long-term co-founder relationships built on shared values are crucial for resilience.
- While urgency is vital in startups, founders should also prioritize developing deep skills and diverse experiences, as mastery and prior knowledge often contribute to later success.
- There is no single mold for a successful entrepreneur; individuals can start companies at various life stages and backgrounds.

## Notable Claims

- Starting a startup and applying to Y Combinator changed Garry Tan's life and connected him with close friends and collaborators. Evidence: Garry Tan's personal testimony at the beginning of the video.
- Saying no to Peter Thiel's invitation to join Palantir as their first software engineer was one of Garry Tan's biggest mistakes. Evidence: Garry Tan's personal reflection on a past career decision.
- Soylent's controversial name, a reference to 'Soylent Green,' generated significant earned media and viral attention, despite initial advice to change it. Evidence: John Coogan's account of Rob's blog posts, the name's polarizing effect, and Soylent's appearance on the Colbert Report.
- Y Combinator looks for founders who are fully committed to their startup and will pursue it even if rejected, demonstrating a 'prove you're wrong' attitude. Evidence: John Coogan's story of his first YC rejection due to a 'one foot in, one foot out' co-founder, and Garry Tan's advice on the 'correct answer' to a rejection scenario.
- A startup's initial team needs complementary skills to cover essential areas (e.g., software engineering, design, product management, sales) to reach its first milestone. Evidence: Garry Tan's explanation of YC's team requirements, stating that a startup usually needs five skills covered by two or three co-founders.
- While urgency is important for startups, founders also need to develop mastery and skills, often through diverse prior experiences, rather than rushing into entrepreneurship without preparation. Evidence: Garry Tan's reference to Ryan Peterson (Flexport) gaining 10,000 hours of experience, Steve Jobs' calligraphy story, and John Coogan's advice to middle/high schoolers to build skills before dropping out.
