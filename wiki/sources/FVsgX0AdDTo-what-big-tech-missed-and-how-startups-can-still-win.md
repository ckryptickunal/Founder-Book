# What Big Tech Missed And How Startups Can Still Win

## Summary

Alexandre Lebrun, a serial entrepreneur and Y Combinator alumnus, discusses his journey from early AI ventures like Wit AI (sold to Facebook) to co-founding Amilabs with Yann LeCun. Amilabs is pursuing 'world models,' an ambitious and capital-intensive approach to AI that learns directly from real-world sensory data, contrasting with Large Language Models (LLMs) that learn from text. Lebrun highlights the limitations of LLMs for common sense and real-world interaction, positioning world models as superior for robotics and high-dimensional problems. He also reflects on the challenges and opportunities for startups versus big tech, the 'cost of expectations' from massive funding rounds (Amilabs raised 1.2 billion euros), and the importance of ambition and a clear long-term vision for founders.

## Key Ideas

- The true cost of raising significant capital is the expectations it creates, not just dilution.
- World models, which learn directly from real-world sensory data, offer a path to AI with common sense and direct experience, unlike LLMs that learn from human-written text.
- LLMs are well-suited for language-first, low-dimensional, and symbolic problems (e.g., math, programming), but struggle with high-dimensional, noisy, and long-horizon real-world tasks.
- Startups have a crucial advantage over large tech companies in their ability to take significant risks and pursue ambitious, early-stage technologies.
- Building foundational AI models (like world models) requires immense capital for compute resources (GPUs) and top talent.
- Entrepreneurs, especially in Europe, should cultivate greater ambition and focus on a narrow problem with a very big, long-term vision.
- Direct experience and 'grounding' are essential for machines to truly understand concepts, rather than just manipulating symbols about them.

## Notable Claims

- The real cost of raising 1.2 billion euros for Amilabs was not dilution, but the expectations created. Evidence: Alexandre Lebrun's direct statement to his team and the interviewer, emphasizing the pressure to deliver results within a timeframe.
- LLMs do not lead to Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). Evidence: Alexandre Lebrun states that 'most people agree that LLMs don't lead to a general intelligence' and notes that those who promised AGI with LLMs have changed the definition or stopped using the term.
- World models will be vastly superior for high-dimensional, very noisy, and very long-horizon problems compared to LLMs. Evidence: Lebrun argues that LLMs are designed for low-dimensional, discrete problems, while world models learn directly from the complex real world, making them better suited for tasks like robotics.
- Current robots are 'very very dumb' and cannot operate safely or usefully in open environments. Evidence: Examples of robots dancing and breaking things or accidentally hitting a child are given to illustrate their lack of common sense and adaptability.
- Vision-Language Assistants (VLAs) are a 'very bad hack' for real-world applications. Evidence: Lebrun criticizes VLAs as inaccurate, slow, expensive, and having high latency, making them unsuitable for applications requiring real-time, precise interaction.
- The difficulty of raising money for a startup depends more on whether it's your first time, rather than the amount being raised. Evidence: Lebrun states that fundraising gets easier with subsequent successful ventures, implying experience and track record are key.
- People, especially in Europe, are not ambitious enough and don't take enough risks in entrepreneurship. Evidence: Lebrun's personal reflection and anecdote about Mark Zuckerberg's extreme ambition (proposing to hire 10,000 concierges instead of 100) as a lesson in aiming higher.
