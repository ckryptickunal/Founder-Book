# How to Get Your First 10 Customers

## Summary

Max, a visiting partner at Y Combinator, provides tactical advice for early-stage founders on how to acquire their first 10 customers, drawing insights from dozens of YC founders. The core message emphasizes understanding customer habits, leveraging warm networks, engaging in-person, participating in online communities where customers express pain, and using outbound tools strategically with personalized, non-salesy approaches. The video stresses that the initial customers are secured through unscalable, manual, founder-led efforts that build trust and facilitate learning before scaling outreach.

## Key Ideas

- Before any outreach, deeply understand where your target customer spends their time (online, in-person, communication preferences).
- Cold email and LinkedIn outreach are often ineffective for buyers who don't primarily work on laptops or prioritize their inbox.
- Leverage your personal and second-degree network (friends, former colleagues, classmates) for your first 1-3 customers, as they buy based on trust in the founder.
- Show up in person (conferences, micro-events, uninvited office visits) for customers 4-10, as this often yields higher conversion rates than remote interactions.
- Actively engage in online communities (Reddit, Facebook groups, industry forums) where your target customers are publicly discussing problems your product solves.
- When using outbound tools like Apollo, Clay, or LinkedIn Premium, frame your initial outreach as seeking advice, mentorship, or product feedback, rather than a direct sales pitch.
- Keep outreach messages concise (under 75 words), include a clear call to action, and ensure they sound human by reading them aloud.
- Offer value upfront (e.g., a free audit, consultation, or specific suggestions) before asking for a meeting to build goodwill.
- Follow up consistently (3-4 times over a couple of weeks) on outreach attempts.
- The journey to the first 10 customers is manual and unscalable, requiring the founder's personal investment to signal genuine care and build trust, which is a unique advantage over established companies.

## Notable Claims

- Most founders default to cold email and LinkedIn outreach because it's easy, but it's often ineffective for many buyer types. Evidence: Many buyers (e.g., property managers, truck dispatchers) don't spend their day on laptops or prioritize email, leading to terrible open and reply rates. A YC founder in a legacy industry closed more in 3 days at a trade show than in 3 months of cold emails.
- The first 2-3 customers almost always come from a founder's warm network. Evidence: A compilation of dozens of YC founder stories showed this pattern 'almost without exception,' citing friends, former colleagues, and classmates as primary sources.
- Early customers buy based on trust in the founder, not just product quality. Evidence: The speaker explains that people are taking a bet on a new product from a new company, and they need to trust the person building it, making the founder's network crucial.
- Prospecting tools only become truly effective after a startup has acquired 10-20 quality customers. Evidence: Many founders waste time setting up outreach tools before working their network, skipping the 'lowest hanging fruit.' The playbook shifts after 10 customers when a refined pitch and case studies exist.
- Showing up in person is one of the most effective, albeit unscalable, tactics for closing early customers. Evidence: Many YC founders physically showed up at customer offices, flew out to meet executives repeatedly, or attended small conferences, reporting higher conversion rates despite the effort and potential rejection.
- Online communities like Reddit, Facebook groups, and industry forums are prime locations to find customers expressing pain points. Evidence: Founders found success by posting product videos on Reddit, DMing commenters in old threads, and responding to complaints on Facebook, leading to sign-ups and customers.
- Effective early outbound outreach is often framed as seeking advice, mentorship, or product review, rather than a direct sales pitch. Evidence: Examples include a founder asking CEOs to be mentors, another conducting 'user research on steroids' by maxing LinkedIn connections with hypotheses, and a Dev Tools founder offering free whiteboarding sessions that required his product.
- Concise outreach copy (under 75 words) with a clear call to action is more effective than long, complex emails. Evidence: Long emails tend to be ignored, and a clear call to action prevents prospects from assuming the worst (e.g., a time-consuming request).
- Offering value upfront before asking for anything significantly increases conversion rates. Evidence: Examples include an API security company offering a vulnerability scan, a mobile onboarding tool providing app suggestions, and a compliance startup preparing a specific audit note before asking for a meeting.
- Customers 1-3 come from your personal network, and customers 4-10 come from doing unscalable, manual things. Evidence: This is presented as a consistent pattern observed across founder surveys, with 'basically no counter examples' for the first few customers.
